Speaking

Skill 1 of 4 — A 7-criteria Application Rubric Evaluation of ICT tools for teaching and learning speaking in ELT contexts.

Speaking

Productive Skill ICT-Enhanced ELT Assessment Skill 1 of 4

About

Speaking is often considered the most challenging skill to assess objectively in ELT — unlike writing, spoken language is transient, spontaneous, and context-dependent. ICT tools for speaking range from video-based response platforms (Flipgrid, VoiceThread) to AI pronunciation feedback systems (ELSA Speak, EnglishCentral) to synchronous video conferencing tools (Zoom, Google Meet). This evaluation applies the 7-criteria Application Rubric framework to assess the quality of these technologies as resources for speaking development and assessment.

7-Criteria Application Rubric Evaluation

An in-depth breakdown of ICT tools for Speaking across the seven evaluation criteria.

01
Criterion 1
🎯 Relevance
✦ Evaluation

ICT tools for speaking are directly relevant to communicative ELT goals. Platforms like Flipgrid and VoiceThread enable asynchronous video recording — learners speak to an authentic audience rather than performing a rehearsed role-play for an empty classroom. ELSA Speak and EnglishCentral target pronunciation and fluency specifically, providing practice at the phoneme, word, and sentence level that textbooks cannot replicate.

Video conferencing tools (Zoom, Google Meet) extend speaking practice beyond the physical classroom — enabling tandem exchanges with international partners, virtual debates, and guest speaker interactions. The breadth of speaking tools available — covering pronunciation, fluency, interaction, and presentation — maps onto all key components of oral communicative competence, justifying a maximum relevance score.

★★★★
02
Criterion 2
💬 Feedback
✦ Evaluation

Feedback on speaking via ICT varies significantly by platform. AI-driven pronunciation tools like ELSA Speak provide detailed, phoneme-level feedback — identifying specific sounds, stress patterns, and intonation errors with visual waveform displays. This is far more precise than a classroom teacher can reliably deliver in real time during group speaking activities. EnglishCentral scores each utterance and highlights mispronounced words immediately after recording.

However, feedback on discourse-level speaking — coherence, argumentation, fluency across extended turns — remains weak across current ICT platforms. No tool adequately evaluates whether a learner's spoken argument is logically structured or communicatively effective beyond pronunciation accuracy. Teacher feedback on Flipgrid recordings is possible but requires significant time. A score of 3 reflects strong pronunciation feedback alongside limited discourse-level feedback.

★★★★
03
Criterion 3
🧠 Thinking Skills
✦ Evaluation

ICT speaking tools span a wide range of cognitive demands. At the lower end, ELSA Speak and pronunciation drills engage primarily repetition and recognition — minimal cognitive processing. At the higher end, tasks designed around video responses (Flipgrid debate threads, VoiceThread discussion prompts) can demand evaluation, synthesis, and persuasion — high-order cognitive operations that require learners to construct and defend spoken arguments.

The cognitive depth achievable via ICT speaking depends entirely on task design. A Flipgrid prompt asking "record yourself agreeing or disagreeing with this controversial statement and explain why" engages critical thinking as effectively as any classroom discussion. Teachers must deliberately design for higher-order outcomes; the tool itself does not enforce cognitive challenge. A score of 3 reflects genuine potential that requires deliberate pedagogical activation.

★★★★
04
Criterion 4
⚙️ Ease of Use
✦ Evaluation

ICT speaking tools present more usability challenges than reading or listening tools because they require functional microphone access, appropriate recording environments, and learner comfort with being recorded. These conditions are not always available: shared devices, noisy classrooms, or cultural reluctance to record one's voice create barriers that text-based tools do not face.

Platforms like Flipgrid are technically simple — recording and sharing a video requires three taps. ELSA Speak onboarding takes under five minutes. However, the social and technical prerequisites (quiet space, working microphone, camera confidence) make the real-world ease of use lower than the interface design suggests. A score of 3 acknowledges intuitive design alongside practical barriers specific to speaking-ICT deployment.

★★★★
05
Criterion 5
🔥 Engagement
✦ Evaluation

ICT speaking tools generate high learner engagement by creating a genuine audience and purpose for speaking. Flipgrid videos are watched and responded to by classmates — creating a social dimension that motivates careful preparation. ELSA Speak's gamified pronunciation challenges (daily streaks, level badges, pronunciation scores) sustain long-term motivation through feedback loops similar to language apps like Duolingo.

For anxious speakers, the asynchronous nature of ICT speaking tasks is a significant engagement advantage: recording alone removes the social pressure of real-time performance in front of peers, dramatically reducing speaking anxiety (Young, 1991). The authentic audience and the ability to re-record until satisfied creates a positive, low-threat speaking environment. Engagement earns a maximum score as one of ICT speaking tools' clearest strengths.

★★★★
06
Criterion 6
🔗 Sharing
✦ Evaluation

Sharing capabilities for ICT speaking tools are good but uneven. Flipgrid integrates directly with Google Classroom and Microsoft Teams, and video responses are shareable via link. VoiceThread allows multimodal commenting (voice, text, video) on shared recordings, creating a collaborative dialogue around speaking performance. Teachers can share discussion threads with colleagues for inter-rater reliability purposes.

However, speaking recordings contain personal data (voice, face) that raises privacy concerns not present for text-based tools. Parental consent for video sharing may be required in some institutional contexts. The portfolio-building capability of recording speaking over time is valuable but requires teachers to manage archives across multiple platforms. Sharing earns a score of 3 — capable but requiring more data governance awareness than other skill tools.

★★★★
07
Criterion 7
🌐 Accessibility & Cost
✦ Evaluation

The cost and accessibility profile of ICT speaking tools is the most varied of the four skill areas. Free options include Flipgrid (now Microsoft Flip, free with Microsoft account), Google Meet, and basic tiers of VoiceThread. However, the most powerful pronunciation feedback tools (ELSA Speak Premium, EnglishCentral Premium) require paid subscriptions that exceed what individual teachers in Indonesian public schools can typically afford.

Accessibility barriers are also the highest of any skill area: reliable microphone hardware, a quiet recording environment, sufficient bandwidth for video, and camera comfort are all prerequisites that disadvantage learners in low-resource contexts. Learners with speech impediments or hearing impairments face additional challenges. These compounding barriers prevent a score above 2 for accessibility and cost in speaking-ICT contexts.

★★★★

Conclusion for Application Rubric Evaluation

A final summary of ICT tools for Speaking as ELT resources, evaluated through the 7-criteria framework.

✦ Final Conclusion
ICT for Speaking: High Engagement Potential with Real-World Constraints
ICT tools for speaking earn a composite score of 21 out of 28 — the lowest in this rubric series, reflecting genuine constraints that are specific to the speaking skill and not weaknesses of any individual platform. The tools that exist are relevant, engaging, and capable of providing strong pronunciation feedback. The barriers — environmental, financial, and social — are contextual rather than technological.

The most important contribution of ICT to speaking development is the authentic audience and the anxiety reduction afforded by asynchronous recording. These two features alone make platforms like Flipgrid and VoiceThread worth integrating into ELT practice. ICT speaking tools work best as supplements to communicative classroom interaction, not replacements for it.

Reflecting and Critical Questions on ICT for Speaking

Reflective and critical questions situating ICT speaking tools within the broader discourse of communicative language teaching and oral assessment theory.

a
How can ICT tools be applied to teach speaking in the ELT classroom?

ICT can support speaking across the preparation-performance-reflection cycle. In preparation, tools like Google Slides or Canva help learners organise their spoken presentations, and ELSA Speak allows private pronunciation rehearsal before performing. In performance, Flipgrid and VoiceThread create an audience for asynchronous video responses, while Zoom and Google Meet enable synchronous group discussion and role-play with remote partners. In reflection, learners can review their own recorded speech — a powerful metacognitive practice that is impossible in face-to-face classrooms without dedicated recording equipment. The self-review loop ICT enables is one of its most underutilised advantages for speaking development.

b
What is the impact of ICT on developing speaking skills?

Research on ICT and speaking consistently identifies two key impacts: anxiety reduction and increased speaking output volume. Asynchronous platforms reduce the social risk of real-time speaking in front of peers, particularly for culturally reserved learners or those with lower proficiency (Hung, 2011). The ability to re-record eliminates the permanence of spoken errors, creating a safer learning environment. Studies on AI pronunciation tools show measurable gains in phoneme accuracy after sustained use of platforms like ELSA Speak (Kim et al., 2018). However, ICT cannot replicate the spontaneity of real interaction — the pragmatic negotiation of meaning in live conversation is a skill that ICT can supplement but not replace.

c
What are the advantages of using ICT for speaking instruction?

Key advantages include: (1) Authentic audience — video responses are viewed by real peers, creating genuine communicative purpose; (2) Anxiety reduction — asynchronous recording removes real-time social pressure; (3) Pronunciation feedback — AI-powered tools provide phoneme-level accuracy that human teachers rarely match; (4) Self-monitoring — learners can watch, listen to, and critique their own speech; (5) Speaking volume increase — ICT homework tasks extend speaking practice beyond the classroom hour; (6) Portfolio building — recordings over time document oral development; (7) Peer feedback culture — Flipgrid and VoiceThread enable structured peer commentary on spoken output.

d
What are the limitations and weaknesses of ICT for speaking?

Key limitations include: (1) Spontaneity loss — asynchronous recording replaces real interaction with a scripted performance; (2) Environmental barriers — quiet recording space and working microphone are not universal; (3) AI feedback limitations — current AI cannot evaluate coherence, appropriateness, or discourse-level fluency; (4) Privacy concerns — video recordings contain personal data requiring careful data governance; (5) Cultural resistance — some learners from cultures with strong face-saving norms resist video self-presentation; (6) Cost — premium pronunciation feedback tools are expensive; (7) Interaction simulation — even live video calls cannot replicate the full pragmatic complexity of face-to-face interaction.

e
Which speaking sub-skills does ICT develop most effectively?

ICT tools are most effective at developing pronunciation — particularly at the segmental level (individual sounds) via AI-powered tools. Fluency is also well supported through extensive practice opportunities that ICT enables outside the classroom. Coherence is developed when learners prepare and record structured responses (presentations, video essays, debate contributions). Vocabulary range benefits from the preparation time that asynchronous recording allows. The sub-skills least effectively developed by ICT are interaction (real-time conversational management, turn-taking, repair strategies) and pragmatic competence (appropriate register, implicature) — both of which require genuine human interlocutors in authentic communicative contexts.
