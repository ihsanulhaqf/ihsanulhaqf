Reading

Receptive Skill ICT-Enhanced ELT Assessment Skill 3 of 4

About

Reading in Indonesian EFL settings has traditionally been dominated by grammar exercises and vocabulary extraction. A rubric-based approach — supported by ICT — repositions comprehension as a multi-layered cognitive process: from identifying main ideas and specific details through making inferences to critically evaluating text. This evaluation applies the same 7-criteria Application Rubric framework used across all four skill areas to assess the quality of ICT tools for reading instruction.

7-Criteria Application Rubric Evaluation

An in-depth breakdown of ICT tools for Reading across the seven evaluation criteria.

01
Criterion 1
🎯 Relevance
✦ Evaluation

ICT tools for reading are comprehensively relevant to ELT goals. Platforms such as CommonLit, NewsELA, ReadWorks, and Rewordify provide graded texts across a wide range of genres — news articles, literary excerpts, informational texts, opinion pieces — that map directly onto the reading skills demanded by academic and real-world contexts. Level-adjustable texts allow teachers to differentiate without creating separate materials.

Beyond text delivery, ICT reading tools support the full taxonomy of reading sub-skills: gist comprehension (via short summary tasks), detail recognition (via targeted question sets), inference (via analysis prompts), vocabulary in context (via embedded glossary and word-hover tools), text structure awareness (via annotation features), and critical reading (via discussion board integration). The breadth of skill coverage earns a maximum relevance score.

★★★★
02
Criterion 2
💬 Feedback
✦ Evaluation

ICT reading tools offer stronger feedback mechanisms than most other skill-based platforms. CommonLit and ReadWorks provide question-level explanations linked directly to textual evidence, teaching learners not only that they were wrong but where in the text the correct answer can be found. This evidence-anchored feedback directly develops the skill of text-based reasoning. Teacher annotation tools in Google Docs allow personalised written feedback on student summaries in real time.

Some platforms now integrate AI-powered feedback on open-ended written responses, evaluating not just accuracy but the quality of reasoning. While still imperfect, this represents a genuine advance over simple multiple-choice marking. The combination of automated and teacher-mediated feedback channels earns reading tools a maximum score for this criterion.

★★★★
03
Criterion 3
🧠 Thinking Skills
✦ Evaluation

Reading is the skill area in which ICT tools most naturally support higher-order thinking. The written nature of the medium allows questions to demand analysis, evaluation, and synthesis in ways that timed listening or speaking tasks cannot. A well-designed CommonLit assignment might move from literal comprehension through character motivation analysis to an evaluative essay prompt — traversing all six levels of Bloom's Taxonomy in a single session.

ICT enables annotation, compare-and-contrast tasks across multiple texts, collaborative discussion boards, and research-linked reading pathways that extend critical literacy far beyond what textbooks support. The reading-ICT combination is arguably the strongest intersection of the four skills for developing higher-order thinking, earning a maximum score.

★★★★
04
Criterion 4
⚙️ Ease of Use
✦ Evaluation

ICT reading platforms are among the most intuitive in the EdTech ecosystem because they closely replicate familiar behaviours: reading on screen is already a daily activity for most learners. Platforms like CommonLit, NewsELA, and Google Docs require minimal onboarding — a teacher can assign a levelled text with comprehension questions in under five minutes. Learners navigate these platforms without training.

Text-to-speech features on platforms like Rewordify or Read&Write extend accessibility to lower-proficiency readers by allowing parallel listening support. Vocabulary tools (hover-to-define, word lists) are embedded without disrupting reading flow. The reading-ICT experience is seamless across device types, and the offline reading mode available in some tools mitigates connectivity concerns. Ease of use is one of reading-ICT's clearest strengths.

★★★★
05
Criterion 5
🔥 Engagement
✦ Evaluation

Reading is inherently quieter and more solitary than speaking or game-based activities, which means the engagement ceiling for ICT reading tools is naturally lower than for gamified platforms. However, ICT significantly raises engagement above traditional printed text delivery. The ability to choose texts by topic and reading level (NewsELA allows learners to select articles on subjects they care about), access multimedia companion content, and engage in peer discussion via collaborative tools like Padlet or Google Classroom greatly increases learner investment.

The engagement limitation is real — sustained silent reading on screen competes with social media for student attention, and screen fatigue is a genuine concern for longer texts. For these reasons, a score of 3 honestly reflects strong but conditional engagement that depends on text selection and task variety.

★★★★
06
Criterion 6
🔗 Sharing
✦ Evaluation

ICT reading tools offer excellent sharing capabilities. Assignments on CommonLit, ReadWorks, and NewsELA can be shared via Google Classroom, direct link, or LMS integration. Google Docs reading tasks can be shared with commenting rights, enabling peer annotation and collaborative reading responses. Teachers can share entire text libraries and question sets with colleagues across the platform's teacher network.

Results analytics — showing which questions caused difficulty, average comprehension scores, and individual learner progress — can be exported and shared with administrators or used for parent communication. The interoperability of reading platforms with Google Workspace and major LMS systems is particularly robust, justifying a maximum sharing score.

★★★★
07
Criterion 7
🌐 Accessibility & Cost
✦ Evaluation

Reading-ICT tools fare better on accessibility and cost than listening platforms, because reading content is less bandwidth-intensive and more platforms offer robust free tiers. ReadWorks, CommonLit (basic tier), and NewsELA (limited) are accessible without cost to teachers and students. Google Docs requires only a free Google account. Rewordify is entirely free with no registration required.

However, levelled reading libraries with full question sets and analytics (CommonLit Premium, NewsELA Plus) require paid institutional subscriptions. Learners with visual impairments benefit from text-to-speech features, but not all platforms offer this uniformly. The better cost profile compared to listening tools, combined with some remaining accessibility limitations, yields a score of 3 — good but not perfect.

★★★★

Conclusion for Application Rubric Evaluation

A final summary of ICT tools for Reading as ELT resources, evaluated through the 7-criteria framework.

✦ Final Conclusion
ICT for Reading: The Strongest Skill-Technology Alignment in ELT
ICT tools for reading earn the highest composite score in this rubric series — 23 out of 28. Reading and ICT are arguably the most naturally aligned skill-technology pairing: the permanence of written text suits digital interaction, and the full range of reading sub-skills — from surface comprehension to critical evaluation — can be scaffolded, practiced, and assessed through well-designed platforms.

The combination of levelled authentic texts, evidence-based feedback, higher-order question design, and seamless LMS integration makes reading-ICT tools indispensable for modern ELT. The main caution is engagement: screen fatigue and the pull of social media compete with sustained reading attention. Teachers should vary text length, genre, and task type to sustain motivation across sessions.
✔ Strongly Recommended as a Core ELT Reading Resource

Reflecting and Critical Questions on ICT for Reading

Reflective and critical questions situating ICT reading tools within the broader discourse of ELT theory and critical literacy.

a
How can ICT tools be applied to teach reading in the ELT classroom?

ICT supports reading across a three-stage pedagogical model. In the pre-reading stage, tools like Mentimeter or Padlet activate schema — learners brainstorm associations with the text topic before reading. During while-reading, annotation tools in Google Docs or Kami allow learners to highlight, comment, and mark comprehension questions directly on the digital text — creating an interactive reading experience that paper cannot replicate. In the post-reading stage, CommonLit discussion boards, Flipgrid video summaries, or collaborative Google Slides encourage learners to synthesise and evaluate what they have read. The ICT-integrated reading lesson is a richer, more dialogic experience than traditional read-and-answer formats.

b
What is the impact of ICT on developing reading skills?

Research shows that ICT-enhanced reading instruction improves comprehension, vocabulary acquisition, and reading strategy use (Mayer, 2009; Warschauer & Grimes, 2007). The key mechanism is interactivity: digital reading tools transform passive text consumption into active engagement through questions, annotations, and multimodal support. Vocabulary tools (hover-definitions, word lists) accelerate incidental vocabulary acquisition by reducing the friction of dictionary lookup. Levelled texts increase reading volume — a critical factor in long-term reading development — by ensuring learners read at an appropriate challenge level. The critical literacy gains enabled by ICT (evaluating source credibility, identifying bias) are perhaps its most distinctive contribution to reading development.

c
What are the advantages of using ICT for reading instruction?

Key advantages include: (1) Authentic, current texts — real-world articles updated daily; (2) Levelled differentiation — same text at multiple Lexile levels for heterogeneous classes; (3) Vocabulary scaffolding — embedded glossary and word-hover tools; (4) Annotation capability — digital highlighting, commenting, peer feedback; (5) Multimedia integration — audio support, video companions, image galleries; (6) Evidence-based feedback — comprehension questions linked to specific text evidence; (7) Analytics — class-level and individual comprehension data; (8) Critical literacy tools — fact-checking features, source evaluation prompts; (9) Low cost — many high-quality platforms have robust free tiers.

d
What are the limitations and weaknesses of ICT for reading?

Key limitations include: (1) Screen fatigue — sustained reading on screens causes eye strain and reduces retention compared to print for some learners; (2) Distraction risk — learners on devices have immediate access to social media and off-task content; (3) Shallow engagement risk — digital reading encourages skimming and scanning rather than deep reading; (4) Comprehension vs. decoding — ICT tools rarely address phonological awareness or word recognition difficulties at the decoding level; (5) Assessment shallowness — automated marking of open-ended questions remains imperfect; (6) Equity — not all learners have personal devices for independent reading at home; (7) Teacher time — curating quality texts and designing meaningful tasks requires significant preparation.

e
Which reading sub-skills does ICT develop most effectively?

ICT reading tools develop the fullest range of sub-skills of any skill-technology pairing. Main idea identification and detail comprehension are developed through structured question sets. Inference and analysis are supported by open-ended prompts and discussion boards that demand text-based justification. Vocabulary in context benefits from embedded vocabulary tools and contextual reading practice. Text structure awareness is supported by graphic organiser tools integrated in platforms like Kami and Google Docs. Critical reading — the most sophisticated sub-skill — is uniquely well served by ICT, which enables fact-checking, cross-text comparison, and source evaluation in ways that print-only materials cannot. The development of critical reading through ICT is the field's greatest current opportunity.
