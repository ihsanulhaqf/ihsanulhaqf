Listening

Skill 2 of 4 — A 7-criteria Application Rubric Evaluation of ICT tools for teaching and learning listening in ELT contexts.

Overview

🎧

Listening

Receptive Skill ICT-Enhanced ELT Assessment Skill 2 of 4

About

Listening is frequently called the "neglected skill" in ELT — yet adults spend an estimated 40–50% of daily communication time listening. This rubric evaluates the quality and suitability of ICT tools used to teach and assess listening in EFL/ELT settings, applying the same 7-criteria Application Rubric Evaluation framework used across all four skill areas. Assessment focuses not on right/wrong answers but on the depth and quality of comprehension processes supported by technology.

7-Criteria Application Rubric Evaluation

An in-depth breakdown of ICT tools for Listening across the seven evaluation criteria used in EdTech assessment.

01
Criterion 1
🎯 Relevance
✦ Evaluation

ICT tools designed for listening instruction are directly and deeply relevant to ELT practice. Platforms such as Edpuzzle, Listenwise, and BBC Learning English provide graded audio and video content aligned with CEFR levels, covering a wide range of genres: news reports, conversations, lectures, interviews, and podcasts. These tools address the full listening skills continuum — from gist comprehension to detailed listening to inferential processing.

Unlike textbook audio tracks, ICT listening tools offer authentic or semi-authentic input at natural speaking rates, exposing learners to connected speech, reduction, and prosodic variation that are essential for real-world listening competence. The relevance to ELT goals — particularly in Indonesian EFL contexts where exposure to authentic spoken English is limited — justifies a maximum score for this criterion.

★★★★
02
Criterion 2
💬 Feedback
✦ Evaluation

Most ICT listening tools provide immediate feedback on comprehension questions — correct/incorrect indicators with the right answer displayed after each response. Platforms like Edpuzzle allow teachers to embed typed feedback on specific questions, giving learners a brief explanation tied to a precise moment in the audio. This is a meaningful step beyond generic right/wrong confirmation.

However, a significant limitation persists: no current ICT listening tool provides real-time feedback on the listening process itself — it cannot detect whether a learner misheard a phoneme, failed to parse connected speech, or guessed correctly without genuine comprehension. The feedback remains output-based rather than process-based, which limits its diagnostic power. A score of 3 reflects strong but incomplete feedback capability.

★★★★
03
Criterion 3
🧠 Thinking Skills
✦ Evaluation

ICT listening tools have genuine potential for higher-order thinking — more so than comparable tools for grammar or vocabulary. Listening to a news podcast and responding to inference questions, for example, requires learners to identify speaker attitude, interpret implied meaning, and distinguish fact from opinion. These are Level 4–6 tasks on Bloom's Taxonomy (analysis, evaluation, creation).

In practice, the depth of thinking demanded depends heavily on how the teacher designs the task. Pre-set question banks in platforms like Listenwise tend toward recall and comprehension (Levels 1–2). Teachers who customise questions, add open-ended post-listening discussion tasks, or pair listening with note-taking significantly extend the cognitive demand. The platform enables but does not automatically provide higher-order thinking — hence a score of 3.

★★★★
04
Criterion 4
⚙️ Ease of Use
✦ Evaluation

ICT tools for listening are generally among the most user-friendly in the EdTech landscape. Learners need only a device, a browser, and (in most cases) a link — no installation required. Playback controls (pause, rewind, speed adjustment) are standard and intuitive. Subtitle and transcript options are accessible with one click, giving learners the autonomy to manage input difficulty.

For teachers, platforms like Edpuzzle allow a video to be cropped, annotated with questions, and shared in under 10 minutes. The learning curve is shallow even for non-technical educators. The one consistent challenge is internet dependency — unreliable connectivity in some Indonesian schools can interrupt streaming audio, though downloaded content partially mitigates this. A perfect score is warranted for ease of use across typical deployment conditions.

★★★★
05
Criterion 5
🔥 Engagement
✦ Evaluation

Listening tasks supported by ICT are inherently more engaging than traditional audio-cassette or CD-based delivery. Video-based platforms (Edpuzzle, YouTube with interactive overlays) add a visual channel that reduces cognitive fatigue and increases interest. The ability to pause, replay, and self-pace gives learners agency that is motivationally significant — particularly for anxious or lower-proficiency learners.

However, listening tasks are by nature quieter and more inward-facing than gamified tools like Kahoot!, and their engagement ceiling is correspondingly lower. Engagement is also material-dependent: a well-chosen podcast on a topic students care about will generate far more engagement than a textbook dialogue, regardless of the delivery platform. Sustainable engagement depends on content curation skill, which is the teacher's responsibility, not the tool's. A score of 3 reflects strong but context-dependent engagement.

★★★★
06
Criterion 6
🔗 Sharing
✦ Evaluation

Most ICT listening platforms support robust sharing. Edpuzzle assignments can be shared via Google Classroom, LTI integration with Moodle or Canvas, or a direct link — with student responses tracked in real time. Listenwise materials can be assigned as homework with automated completion tracking. Teachers can share their custom listening tasks with colleagues within the platform's teacher network.

One limitation: platforms that rely on licensed audio content (e.g., Listenwise with NPR content) may restrict sharing outside the platform or require both parties to hold a subscription. YouTube-based tasks are freely shareable but lack the built-in question-embedding of dedicated platforms. The overall sharing ecosystem is good but with licensing caveats that prevent a perfect score. A score of 3 is appropriate.

★★★★
07
Criterion 7
🌐 Accessibility & Cost
✦ Evaluation

The accessibility picture for ICT listening tools is mixed. Free-tier options exist (Edpuzzle free, YouTube, BBC Learning English, ESL Pod), and these are usable for basic classroom listening tasks. However, the richest platforms for curated, graded listening content (Listenwise Premium, EnglishCentral Premium) require paid subscriptions that are prohibitive for many Indonesian public school teachers operating without institutional support.

Accessibility is also complicated by bandwidth: high-quality audio streaming requires a reliable connection that is not universally available. Learners with hearing impairments may benefit from subtitle and transcript options, but these are not universally available across all platforms. The combination of cost barriers and connectivity constraints makes 2 out of 4 a fair and honest score for this criterion.

★★★★

Conclusion for Application Rubric Evaluation

A final summary of ICT tools for Listening as ELT resources, evaluated through the 7-criteria framework.

✦ Final Conclusion
ICT for Listening: Authentic Input with Thoughtful Integration
ICT tools for listening instruction earn a strong overall score of 22 out of 28 on the Application Rubric Evaluation. They are highly relevant to ELT goals, easy to use, and capable of delivering authentic, graded listening input that textbooks alone cannot provide. Their greatest strengths lie in relevance and usability — bringing real-world spoken English into the classroom with minimal technical barriers.

The key limitations are the depth of feedback, the cost of premium platforms, and the fact that higher-order thinking must be deliberately designed by the teacher rather than generated automatically by the tool. Used strategically — as a vehicle for authentic input, comprehension practice, and inference-building tasks — ICT listening tools are among the most pedagogically valuable digital resources available to ELT practitioners.

Reflecting and Critical Questions on ICT for Listening

Reflective and critical questions situating ICT listening tools within the broader discourse of ELT theory and practice.

a
How can ICT tools be applied to teach listening in the ELT classroom?

ICT tools can be applied across all three stages of a listening lesson. In the pre-listening stage, platforms like Padlet or Mentimeter can activate schema by eliciting predictions about the topic. During while-listening, Edpuzzle or PlayPosit embed pause-and-answer questions at precise timestamps, forcing active engagement rather than passive reception. In the post-listening stage, Flipgrid allows learners to record spoken summaries, and Google Docs enables collaborative note-taking. The sequenced three-stage model applies naturally to ICT-mediated listening, with each stage supported by a different category of tool.

b
What is the impact of ICT on developing listening skills?

Research consistently shows that ICT-enhanced listening instruction produces measurable gains in comprehension, vocabulary recognition, and listening strategy use (Vandergrift & Goh, 2012). The key mechanism is increased exposure to authentic input — learners encounter natural speech rates, connected speech, and prosodic variation that scripted textbook audio cannot replicate. ICT also reduces listening anxiety: the ability to replay, pause, and control pace gives learners agency that significantly lowers affective barriers to engagement (Krashen, 1982). When paired with explicit strategy instruction, ICT listening tools support the development of metacognitive listening awareness — learners become conscious of how they listen, not just what they hear.

c
What are the advantages of using ICT for listening instruction?

Key advantages include: (1) Authentic input — real-world audio/video at natural pace; (2) Learner autonomy — replay, pause, speed control, subtitles; (3) Differentiation — content graded by CEFR level, accent, and genre; (4) Embedded comprehension tasks — interactive questions without paper; (5) Immediate feedback — automated response tracking; (6) Analytics — teachers see which questions caused difficulty and where students paused; (7) Blended learning compatibility — homework listening with asynchronous tracking; (8) Reduced anxiety — private, self-paced practice reduces performance pressure.

d
What are the limitations and weaknesses of ICT for listening?

Key limitations include: (1) Process invisibility — ICT can measure listening outputs but not the cognitive process of listening itself; (2) Connectivity dependency — unreliable internet disrupts streaming tasks; (3) Content quality variability — teachers must curate carefully; free platforms include low-quality material; (4) Passive risk — without task design, learners may watch rather than listen actively; (5) Transcript over-reliance — subtitles can become a crutch that bypasses listening processing; (6) Assessment shallowness — most tools only measure comprehension outcomes, not listening strategies; (7) Cost — premium curated platforms are expensive for individual teachers.

e
Which listening sub-skills does ICT develop most effectively?

ICT tools are most effective at developing gist comprehension (identifying the main idea of a spoken text) and detail recognition (answering specific factual questions). They are also valuable for developing vocabulary in context, since learners encounter unknown words in authentic spoken co-text. With deliberate task design, ICT can support inference and critical listening — distinguishing fact from opinion, identifying speaker attitude, interpreting implication. The most underserved sub-skill is note-taking, which requires learners to write while listening simultaneously — a skill that most platforms do not scaffold or assess directly.
