Writing

Writing

Productive Skill ICT-Enhanced ELT Assessment Skill 4 of 4

About

Writing is the only productive skill that leaves a permanent, reviewable record — making it uniquely suited to ICT integration. Tools ranging from Google Docs (collaborative drafting) to Grammarly (automated feedback) to Padlet (multimodal composition) transform writing from a solitary, teacher-only-read product into a dynamic, iterative, socially embedded practice. This evaluation applies the 7-criteria Application Rubric framework to assess how well ICT serves writing development in ELT contexts.

7-Criteria Application Rubric Evaluation

An in-depth breakdown of ICT tools for Writing across the seven evaluation criteria.

01
Criterion 1
🎯 Relevance
✦ Evaluation

ICT tools for writing are among the most directly relevant to ELT practice of any digital category. Writing is the one skill that ICT has most thoroughly transformed: from word processing (replacing longhand with editable digital text) to collaborative drafting (Google Docs enabling real-time co-writing) to automated feedback (Grammarly identifying grammar, punctuation, and style errors instantly) to genre-specific platforms (Padlet for multimodal composition, Blogger for authentic publishing).

These tools map precisely onto the writing skill dimensions most valued in ELT: content generation, structural organisation, grammatical accuracy, lexical precision, cohesion, and task achievement. The range and precision of writing-ICT alignment justify a maximum relevance score — writing is the skill that ICT serves with the greatest breadth and depth.

★★★★
02
Criterion 2
💬 Feedback
✦ Evaluation

Writing-ICT offers the richest feedback ecosystem of any skill-technology pairing. Grammarly provides real-time, layered feedback: grammar corrections with explanations, style suggestions, clarity scores, engagement ratings, and tone indicators — all linked to specific words or sentences in the text. The Hemingway Editor grades overall readability and highlights passive voice, complex sentences, and weak adverbs. Google Docs commenting allows asynchronous teacher and peer feedback directly embedded in the text, visible to learners alongside their own writing.

For academic writing, tools like Turnitin combine plagiarism detection with grammar and citation feedback. The combination of automated micro-feedback (Grammarly) and human macro-feedback (teacher comments in Google Docs) creates a layered feedback architecture that is uniquely powerful for writing development. No other skill area offers equivalent feedback richness via ICT, earning a maximum score.

★★★★
03
Criterion 3
🧠 Thinking Skills
✦ Evaluation

Writing is intrinsically the most cognitively demanding of the four skills — it requires planning, drafting, revising, and evaluating simultaneously. ICT amplifies all of these processes. Mind-mapping tools (Miro, Google Jamboard) support the pre-writing planning stage by externalising idea organisation. Version history in Google Docs makes the revision process visible and teachable. Peer review via shared documents transforms evaluation into a social, dialogic practice.

Research-integrated writing tasks — where learners search, evaluate, synthesise, and cite sources within a single ICT workflow — engage all six levels of Bloom's Taxonomy. Writing is also the skill most naturally suited to argumentation, critical evaluation, and original creation — the highest cognitive orders. ICT amplifies rather than limits this potential, justifying a maximum score for thinking skills in writing contexts.

★★★★
04
Criterion 4
⚙️ Ease of Use
✦ Evaluation

ICT writing tools are among the most universally familiar digital tools available — word processing is among the first digital skills learners acquire, and Google Docs requires no training whatsoever for anyone with basic computer literacy. The transition from paper writing to ICT writing has a near-zero learning curve for most learners above primary school level.

Grammarly installs as a browser extension or app in under two minutes. Padlet accounts are created in seconds. The Google Workspace ecosystem (Docs, Slides, Sheets) is accessible through any browser on any device with no installation. Even more specialised tools like Hemingway Editor require no account — learners paste text into the web interface immediately. The ease of use for writing-ICT tools is consistently the highest across all four skill areas, meriting a maximum score.

★★★★
05
Criterion 5
🔥 Engagement
✦ Evaluation

Writing engagement via ICT depends heavily on audience and purpose. The single most powerful engagement driver for writing is publishing to a real audience: a class blog, a Padlet wall, a collaborative wiki, or a shared Google Doc that peers will read and comment on. When learners know their writing will be read beyond the teacher, the quality and care they invest increases markedly. ICT enables this authentic publishing dimension that handwritten essays fundamentally cannot.

However, writing remains a cognitively demanding and solitary activity whose engagement profile is inherently lower than gamified or social activities. Grammarly's feedback can feel mechanical, and Google Docs offers no intrinsic motivational elements beyond the task itself. For sustained engagement, writing tasks must be purposeful, topic-relevant, and genuinely shared. ICT enables but does not guarantee engagement — hence a score of 3.

★★★★
06
Criterion 6
🔗 Sharing
✦ Evaluation

Writing-ICT tools excel at sharing. Google Docs can be shared with view, comment, or edit rights in one click — enabling teacher feedback, peer review, and collaborative authorship simultaneously. Padlet walls are publicly accessible via link. Class blogs publish to the open internet. Writing portfolios built in Google Sites or Seesaw can be shared with parents, administrators, or future employers as evidence of development over time.

The revision history feature in Google Docs is a unique sharing advantage: it documents every change made to a document, making the writing process itself shareable as a learning artefact. Teachers can review not just the final product but the entire drafting journey, assessing process alongside outcome. This level of process visibility is unique to ICT-mediated writing and earns a maximum sharing score.

★★★★
07
Criterion 7
🌐 Accessibility & Cost
✦ Evaluation

Writing-ICT has the best cost profile of the four skill areas. Google Docs, Google Classroom, Padlet (limited free tier), Hemingway Editor, and basic Grammarly are all accessible at no cost. The Google Workspace for Education suite is free for qualifying institutions globally, covering the full writing workflow from drafting to peer review to teacher feedback. This makes writing-ICT the most financially accessible skill technology available.

Limitations exist: Grammarly Premium (with advanced style and clarity features) and Turnitin (plagiarism detection with detailed feedback) require institutional subscriptions. Learners with dyslexia or motor disabilities may find sustained typing challenging, though text-to-speech-to-text tools partially address this. Internet dependency for cloud-based tools remains a constraint in low-connectivity settings. These limitations prevent a perfect score, but the excellent baseline of free, high-quality tools justifies a 3 out of 4.

★★★★

Conclusion for Application Rubric Evaluation

A final summary of ICT tools for Writing as ELT resources, evaluated through the 7-criteria framework.

✦ Final Conclusion
ICT for Writing: The Most Comprehensive Skill-Technology Partnership in ELT
ICT tools for writing earn a composite score of 24 out of 28 — tied with Kahoot! for the highest score in this rubric series and the strongest result among the four language skills. Writing and ICT are extraordinarily well matched: the permanence of written text, the iterability of the drafting process, the layered feedback needs, and the social potential of publishing all align perfectly with what ICT tools do best.

Google Docs alone transforms writing pedagogy — enabling real-time collaboration, embedded teacher commentary, peer review, and process documentation in a single free tool. When paired with grammar feedback (Grammarly), readability analysis (Hemingway), and authentic publication (class blog, Padlet), the ICT writing toolkit is as comprehensive and pedagogically grounded as any resource available to ELT practitioners. Writing-ICT is not a supplement to good writing instruction — it is the infrastructure for it.
✔ Strongly Recommended as a Core ELT Writing Resource

Reflecting and Critical Questions on ICT for Writing

Reflective and critical questions situating ICT writing tools within the broader discourse of process writing theory, genre pedagogy, and academic literacy.

a
How can ICT tools be applied to teach writing in the ELT classroom?

ICT supports the full process writing cycle. In pre-writing, mind-mapping tools (Miro, Coggle) help learners plan ideas visually, and shared Padlets allow collaborative brainstorming. In drafting, Google Docs enables collaborative writing, live teacher monitoring, and simultaneous peer input. In revision, Grammarly provides automated structural feedback while Google Docs comments enable targeted teacher guidance on specific sentences. In editing, Hemingway Editor helps learners simplify complex structures and reduce passive voice. In publishing, class blogs, Google Sites, or Padlet walls provide an authentic audience beyond the classroom. This ICT-supported process model gives learners agency, transparency, and iterative practice that paper writing cannot replicate.

b
What is the impact of ICT on developing writing skills?

Research consistently identifies ICT's greatest writing impact as improving revision behaviour — the willingness to edit, restructure, and improve drafts (Bangert-Drowns, 1993). Word processing lowers the cost of revision: erasing and rewriting on paper is laborious; editing digitally is instantaneous. Automated feedback tools (Grammarly) increase grammar awareness by naming and explaining errors rather than simply marking them. Authentic publishing to real audiences measurably improves writing quality through heightened writer motivation (Warschauer, 2006). Collaborative writing via Google Docs develops strategic thinking about audience, register, and organisation through the negotiation of shared text. ICT's impact on writing is the most consistently positive across ELT research compared to any other skill area.

c
What are the advantages of using ICT for writing instruction?

Key advantages include: (1) Process visibility — revision history documents the entire writing journey; (2) Layered feedback — automated grammar + human discourse-level feedback in one workflow; (3) Collaboration — real-time co-writing, peer review, and co-editing; (4) Authentic audience — publishing to blogs/Padlets motivates quality; (5) Revision ease — digital editing removes the barrier to substantive rewrites; (6) Multimodality — text, image, video, and link can be combined in digital compositions; (7) Portfolio building — document history creates evidence of long-term development; (8) Accessibility — text-to-speech, spelling tools, and grammar aids support diverse learners; (9) Low cost — Google Workspace provides comprehensive tools for free.

d
What are the limitations and weaknesses of ICT for writing?

Key limitations include: (1) Over-reliance on automated feedback — learners may accept Grammarly suggestions without understanding them, reducing genuine learning; (2) Plagiarism and AI generation — ICT makes copying and AI-generated text generation trivially easy, complicating assessment integrity; (3) Handwriting neglect — exclusive digital writing may impair handwriting fluency required in examinations; (4) Distraction — writing on a connected device creates constant distraction temptation; (5) Depth vs. speed — typing speed does not correlate with writing quality; fast typists may produce more but not better text; (6) Teacher feedback burden — Google Docs commenting can increase teacher workload if not managed strategically; (7) Digital divide — home access to devices for homework writing tasks disadvantages lower-income learners.

e
Which writing sub-skills does ICT develop most effectively?

ICT is most effective at developing grammar accuracy — automated tools provide more consistent, comprehensive error identification than any teacher can deliver across an entire class's drafts simultaneously. Cohesion and coherence benefit from revision tools and the visibility of document structure. Vocabulary range is supported by built-in thesaurus, vocabulary suggestion tools, and the ease of deleting and replacing weak word choices digitally. Task achievement is supported by digital rubric checklists learners can consult while writing. The sub-skill least effectively developed by ICT alone is content and ideas — generating original, substantive, well-reasoned content requires domain knowledge, intellectual engagement, and creative thinking that no tool can substitute. This is where teacher instruction, discussion, and reading input remain irreplaceable.
