Speaking

Speaking

Productive Skill ICT-Enhanced ELT Assessment Skill 1 of 4

About

Speaking is often considered the most challenging skill to assess objectively in ELT — unlike writing, spoken language is transient, spontaneous, and context-dependent. ICT tools for speaking range from video-based response platforms (Flipgrid, VoiceThread) to AI pronunciation feedback systems (ELSA Speak, EnglishCentral) to synchronous video conferencing tools (Zoom, Google Meet). This evaluation applies the 7-criteria Application Rubric framework to assess the quality of these technologies as resources for speaking development and assessment.

7-Criteria Application Rubric Evaluation

An in-depth breakdown of ICT tools for Speaking across the seven evaluation criteria.

01
Criterion 1
🎯 Relevance
✦ Evaluation

ESL Fast's structure — a library of short, topic-organised dialogues paired with native-speaker audio — maps directly onto the Listen → Repeat → Perform sequence used in this Grade 7 lesson design. Rather than treating speaking as an isolated skill, the dialogue format lets pronunciation, rhythm, and turn-taking be rehearsed through an authentic-sounding exchange before students ever have to produce language unaided.

The topic-based extension — choosing a theme such as Friendship or Daily Life and asking students to build an original conversation on the same pattern — pushes the activity from controlled practice into genuine communicative output, exactly the kind of meaning-focused, personally relevant task that early-adolescent learners respond to. Because every stage of the sequence traces back to one authentic dialogue, relevance to communicative speaking goals stays high throughout.

★★★★
02
Criterion 2
💬 Feedback
✦ Evaluation

This is where ESL Fast's simplicity becomes a real limitation. The platform has no recording function, no speech recognition, and no scoring — unlike ELSA Speak or EnglishCentral, it was never built to evaluate spoken output. The only feedback built into the tool itself is implicit: students compare their own repeated speech against the native-speaker audio model and self-correct by ear.

In practice, all explicit feedback in this lesson comes from the teacher, delivered live during the Perform stage as pairs act out the dialogue, and from classmates watching the performance. That feedback depends entirely on the teacher's attention and time rather than anything the platform guarantees, and it cannot match the phoneme-level precision of AI-driven alternatives. A score of 2 reflects a workable but minimal feedback loop — one of the more honest trade-offs of choosing a free, low-tech resource over a dedicated speaking app.

★★★★
03
Criterion 3
🧠 Thinking Skills
✦ Evaluation

The Listen → Repeat stage sits at the lower end of the cognitive scale: it is recognition and imitation, reproducing a model rather than generating language. On its own, this stage would justify only a modest score.

The two later stages raise the ceiling considerably. The topic-based task asks students to abstract the underlying pattern of a model dialogue and populate it with their own ideas — a genuine act of synthesis, not repetition. The comprehension questions add a further layer: when a teacher repurposes them as oral discussion prompts rather than written checks, students move into interpretation and justification. Because the higher-order thinking is reachable but depends on the teacher choosing to extend the questions into discussion, a score of 3 reflects strong potential rather than a built-in guarantee.

★★★★
04
Criterion 4
⚙️ Ease of Use
✦ Evaluation

ESL Fast asks for almost nothing from teacher or student: no account, no installation, no specialised hardware beyond a way to play audio. For a Grade 7 class where not every student owns a personal device, this matters — the dialogue and its audio can be played from a single classroom computer or phone while the actual speaking happens off-screen, in pairs.

Because the performing stage is live and unrecorded, the lesson also avoids the usual technical friction of speaking-ICT tools: no uploads, no microphone permissions, no app crashes mid-recording. The trade-off — taken up again under Sharing — is that nothing is captured for later review. For sheer operational simplicity in a typical Indonesian junior-high classroom, this scores higher than most dedicated speaking apps.

★★★★
05
Criterion 5
🔥 Engagement
✦ Evaluation

Performing a dialogue in pairs in front of classmates creates a real, immediate audience — closer to a small stage moment than a private exercise — and the topic choices (Friendship, Daily Life) sit close to a 12-13 year-old's actual social world, which helps motivation at an age where relevance to one's own life matters more than novelty.

What the lesson lacks is the app-side gamification that sustains engagement in tools like ELSA Speak or Duolingo — no streaks, points, or badges, since ESL Fast is a plain content library rather than a gamified learning app. The engagement here comes almost entirely from how the teacher stages the activity, not from the platform itself, which is why the score sits just below maximum.

★★★★
06
Criterion 6
🔗 Sharing
✦ Evaluation

This is the weakest criterion for ESL Fast in this design. The platform has no built-in recording, posting, or portfolio feature — dialogues are read from the page, not captured through it — so there is no digital trace of a student's performance once a pair finishes.

"Sharing" here exists only in its most basic, synchronous sense: the performance is shared live with the class as an audience, nothing more. Compared to platforms like Flipgrid, where every recording becomes a reviewable, archivable artifact, this is a real gap — fine for in-the-moment communicative practice, but it leaves the teacher no portfolio evidence of progress over time unless a separate recording step is added.

★★★★
07
Criterion 7
🌐 Accessibility & Cost
✦ Evaluation

Where ESL Fast loses ground on feedback and sharing, it gains it back fully here. The site is entirely free, with no premium tier, no subscription, and no account wall — a meaningful point in Indonesian public-school contexts where the paid tiers of ELSA Speak or EnglishCentral are often out of reach for an individual teacher's budget.

Bandwidth demands are minimal — short audio clips and text load even on a slow connection — and because the speaking practice itself happens face-to-face rather than through a recording upload, no student needs a personal device, microphone, or data plan to take part. For a low-resource setting, this is about as favourable as a speaking resource gets.

★★★★

Conclusion for Application Rubric Evaluation

A final summary of ICT tools for Speaking as ELT resources, evaluated through the 7-criteria framework.

✦ Final Conclusion
ICT for Speaking: High Engagement Potential with Real-World Constraints
ICT tools for speaking earn a composite score of 21 out of 28 — ESL Fast as the basis for this Grade 7 speaking sequence earns a composite score of 22 out of 28. A clear pattern runs through all seven criteria: wherever the result depends on what the teacher does with the material — staging a live performance, turning a comprehension question into a discussion prompt, picking a topic students actually care about — the score is high. Wherever it depends on what the platform does automatically — scoring pronunciation, capturing a recording, sustaining engagement through gamification — the score drops, because ESL Fast was never built as a speaking tool in the first place.

That trade-off makes sense for a Grade 7 classroom with limited devices and budget: the Listen → Repeat → Perform sequence converts a free dialogue-and-audio library into genuine, low-anxiety communicative speaking practice, at the cost of any digital record of how that speaking actually sounded. The platform supplies the model; the teacher supplies the feedback, the audience, and the extension into original, topic-based conversation. Used this way, ESL Fast functions less as a speaking app and more as a speaking scaffold — and for that purpose, it earns its place in the lesson.

Reflecting and Critical Questions on ICT for Speaking

Reflective and critical questions situating ICT speaking tools within the broader discourse of communicative language teaching and oral assessment theory.

a
How can ICT tools be applied to teach speaking in the ELT classroom?

ICT can support speaking across the preparation-performance-reflection cycle. In preparation, tools like Google Slides or Canva help learners organise their spoken presentations, and ELSA Speak allows private pronunciation rehearsal before performing. In performance, Flipgrid and VoiceThread create an audience for asynchronous video responses, while Zoom and Google Meet enable synchronous group discussion and role-play with remote partners. In reflection, learners can review their own recorded speech — a powerful metacognitive practice that is impossible in face-to-face classrooms without dedicated recording equipment. The self-review loop ICT enables is one of its most underutilised advantages for speaking development.

b
What is the impact of ICT on developing speaking skills?

Research on ICT and speaking consistently identifies two key impacts: anxiety reduction and increased speaking output volume. Asynchronous platforms reduce the social risk of real-time speaking in front of peers, particularly for culturally reserved learners or those with lower proficiency (Hung, 2011). The ability to re-record eliminates the permanence of spoken errors, creating a safer learning environment. Studies on AI pronunciation tools show measurable gains in phoneme accuracy after sustained use of platforms like ELSA Speak (Kim et al., 2018). However, ICT cannot replicate the spontaneity of real interaction — the pragmatic negotiation of meaning in live conversation is a skill that ICT can supplement but not replace.

c
What are the advantages of using ICT for speaking instruction?

Key advantages include: (1) Authentic audience — video responses are viewed by real peers, creating genuine communicative purpose; (2) Anxiety reduction — asynchronous recording removes real-time social pressure; (3) Pronunciation feedback — AI-powered tools provide phoneme-level accuracy that human teachers rarely match; (4) Self-monitoring — learners can watch, listen to, and critique their own speech; (5) Speaking volume increase — ICT homework tasks extend speaking practice beyond the classroom hour; (6) Portfolio building — recordings over time document oral development; (7) Peer feedback culture — Flipgrid and VoiceThread enable structured peer commentary on spoken output.

d
What are the limitations and weaknesses of ICT for speaking?

Key limitations include: (1) Spontaneity loss — asynchronous recording replaces real interaction with a scripted performance; (2) Environmental barriers — quiet recording space and working microphone are not universal; (3) AI feedback limitations — current AI cannot evaluate coherence, appropriateness, or discourse-level fluency; (4) Privacy concerns — video recordings contain personal data requiring careful data governance; (5) Cultural resistance — some learners from cultures with strong face-saving norms resist video self-presentation; (6) Cost — premium pronunciation feedback tools are expensive; (7) Interaction simulation — even live video calls cannot replicate the full pragmatic complexity of face-to-face interaction.

e
Which speaking sub-skills does ICT develop most effectively?

ICT tools are most effective at developing pronunciation — particularly at the segmental level (individual sounds) via AI-powered tools. Fluency is also well supported through extensive practice opportunities that ICT enables outside the classroom. Coherence is developed when learners prepare and record structured responses (presentations, video essays, debate contributions). Vocabulary range benefits from the preparation time that asynchronous recording allows. The sub-skills least effectively developed by ICT are interaction (real-time conversational management, turn-taking, repair strategies) and pragmatic competence (appropriate register, implicature) — both of which require genuine human interlocutors in authentic communicative contexts.
